# LUCID-SCI
## Precision-Preserving Scientific Writing Skill

## 1. Mission

Transform complex scientific content into prose that a serious non-specialist can follow without changing the scientific meaning, weakening methodological detail, exaggerating certainty or adopting a colloquial voice.

The skill must reduce **extraneous difficulty**, not **intrinsic scientific complexity**.

### Governing maxim

> Simplify the route to the idea, never the idea itself.

## 2. Target register

The default register is **cross-disciplinary scholarly prose**.

Assume that the reader:

- is intelligent and scientifically literate;
- may understand research design and quantitative evidence in general;
- may not know the field's local terminology, acronyms, conventions or current debates;
- wants the main argument quickly but must be able to inspect the technical basis;
- should not be patronised, entertained with slang or forced to decode status-signalling prose.

This register lies between specialist shorthand and public-facing plain language.

| Register | Assumed knowledge | Technical terms | Quantitative detail | Tone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Specialist shorthand | High and field-specific | Often unexplained | Full | Dense, compressed |
| **LUCID-SCI target** | General scientific literacy | Retained when exact; bridged on first use | Full and interpreted | Formal, direct, calm |
| Public plain language | Little technical background | Mostly translated | Selective | Accessible, explanatory |
| Popular or street language | Variable | Usually avoided | Often reduced | Conversational, informal |

## 3. Non-negotiable scientific invariants

Before rewriting, construct a **Semantic Checksum**. These elements must remain unchanged unless the author explicitly corrects them.

1. **Claim class**: descriptive, associational, predictive, causal, mechanistic, evaluative or normative.
2. **Question or estimand**: exactly what is being estimated, compared or explained.
3. **Population**: who or what the statement applies to.
4. **Setting**: where and under what context.
5. **Exposure, intervention or index condition**.
6. **Comparator or counterfactual**.
7. **Outcome** and how it was defined.
8. **Time zero, follow-up period and temporal ordering**.
9. **Study design and evidence source**.
10. **Effect measure or statistical quantity**.
11. **Uncertainty**: confidence or credible interval, standard error, range, sensitivity or qualitative uncertainty.
12. **Assumptions and adjustment set**, when material to interpretation.
13. **Scope conditions and exclusions**.
14. **Limitations and plausible alternative explanations**.
15. **Strength of inference** and any boundary on generalisation.

### Hard rule

A clearer sentence that changes any invariant is an incorrect edit.

## 4. The LUCID sequence

### L: Locate the reader, purpose and required action

Record:

- primary reader;
- secondary reader;
- what each already knows;
- what each needs to understand;
- what they should conclude or do after reading;
- which details must remain in the main text and which can move to methods, notes or supplementary material.

Do not begin by substituting simpler words. Begin by deciding what the reader needs and in what order.

### U: Unpack the scientific spine

Reduce the content to seven questions:

1. What problem exists?
2. What exactly is unknown or contested?
3. What did the study or analysis do?
4. What did it find?
5. How large and uncertain was the finding?
6. What does the finding mean?
7. What should not be inferred?

Write one sentence for each question before drafting the polished text.

### C: Construct the information path

Order information so the reader does not have to reverse-engineer the argument.

Default sequence:

**known context → specific gap → study action → main result → interpretation → boundary**

At sentence level, connect familiar information to new information. Place the topic early and the main new point near the sentence end when natural.

### I: Interpret methods, numbers and implications

Never present a method label, coefficient or model output without explaining its role.

Use the pattern:

> We used **[method]**, which **[plain functional explanation]**, to **[scientific purpose]**.

For results, use:

> **[Direction and magnitude in ordinary scientific language]** (**[exact estimate and uncertainty]**), relative to **[comparator]** over **[time]**.

Where possible and valid, provide both absolute and relative quantities. Never invent an absolute effect when the baseline risk is unavailable.

### D: Discipline claims, terminology and tone

Check that:

- verbs match the strength of evidence;
- every essential technical term is accurate;
- unnecessary jargon and acronyms are removed;
- caveats are visible rather than buried;
- implications do not exceed the population, setting, design or follow-up;
- the prose is formal without sounding bureaucratic, theatrical or promotional.

## 5. Terminology protocol: KEEP, BRIDGE, REPLACE, REMOVE

### KEEP

Keep a technical term when it names a concept precisely and no ordinary-language substitute preserves the distinction.

Examples: confounding, mediation, interaction, transportability, calibration, positivity, competing risk, heterogeneity, estimand.

Keeping the term does not remove the duty to explain it.

### BRIDGE

On first use, connect the exact term to its function or meaning.

Preferred forms:

1. **Plain meaning first, technical label second**  
   “We estimated the risk that would have been observed if all eligible children had received each regimen, an approach known as standardisation.”

2. **Technical label plus functional apposition**  
   “We used inverse-probability weighting, which creates a weighted sample in which measured baseline characteristics are more comparable across treatment groups.”

3. **Term plus consequence**  
   “Positivity requires that each treatment remains possible within every covariate pattern included in the comparison. Without this overlap, effects cannot be estimated reliably for those patients.”

4. **Term by contrast**  
   “Prediction asks who is likely to experience an outcome; causal inference asks how that outcome might change under a specified intervention.”

### REPLACE

Replace a term when an ordinary word carries the same meaning with less friction.

- utilise → use
- interrogate data → examine data
- facilitate → help or enable, depending on meaning
- elucidate → identify, explain or clarify
- ameliorate → reduce or improve
- operationalise → define and measure, when that is what occurred
- instantiate → create or represent
- leverage → use
- prior to → before
- in order to → to
- a total of 524 participants → 524 participants
- was found to be associated with → was associated with

### REMOVE

Delete language that signals status but adds no scientific content.

Common examples:

- groundbreaking
- pivotal
- transformative
- unprecedented, unless literally demonstrated
- multifaceted
- holistic
- cutting-edge
- paradigm-shifting
- robust, without naming what was stable and across which checks
- comprehensive, without specifying the coverage
- critically important
- it is important to note that
- the fact that
- in the context of
- with regard to
- underscores the urgent need
- holds considerable promise

## 6. Jargon budget

Jargon is not forbidden. Unmanaged jargon is.

For every specialised term, ask:

1. Is the distinction scientifically necessary?
2. Will the term recur often enough to justify learning it?
3. Can the reader infer its function from the sentence?
4. Is it defined with familiar words rather than another specialist term?
5. Could the term move to the Methods or a glossary without harming the argument?

### House heuristics

These are review triggers, not journal laws.

- Introduce no more than one or two genuinely new technical concepts in a sentence.
- Avoid acronyms used fewer than three times.
- Do not introduce more than two new acronyms in one paragraph.
- Prefer the full term when the acronym saves little space.
- Reintroduce an uncommon term after a long gap if the reader may have forgotten it.
- Do not make the reader learn two labels for the same concept.

## 7. Sentence engineering

### 7.1 One primary job per sentence

A sentence may contain qualifications, but it should have one dominant communicative purpose: context, gap, method, result, interpretation or limitation.

Split a sentence when it contains competing conclusions or when more than one clause deserves emphasis.

### 7.2 Keep subject and verb close

Avoid long interruptions between the grammatical subject and its action.

Weak:

> The association between household wealth and wasting, after adjustment for maternal education, residence, parity, maternal age, sanitation and region, was estimated using...

Better:

> We estimated the association between household wealth and wasting after adjusting for maternal education, residence, parity, maternal age, sanitation and region.

### 7.3 Put the action in the verb

Weak nominalisation:

> We conducted an evaluation of the implementation of the intervention.

Better:

> We evaluated how the intervention was implemented.

Do not remove a nominalisation when it is the recognised name of a scientific construct, such as “mediation” or “standardisation”.

### 7.4 Use concrete grammatical subjects

Prefer the true actor, object or process over empty openings.

Weak:

> It was observed that mortality was higher...

Better:

> Mortality was higher...

Or, when agency matters:

> We observed higher mortality...

### 7.5 Connect old information to new information

Begin with an idea the previous sentence has prepared. End with the point that advances the argument.

Disconnected:

> Maternal height modifies the wealth gradient. Rural residence was included in the model. The predicted risks varied across countries.

Connected:

> The wealth gradient differed by maternal height. This pattern remained after adjustment for rural residence, but its magnitude varied across countries.

### 7.6 Place qualifications where they are easy to attach

Do not make the reader wait until the end of a paragraph to learn that a result was exploratory, unadjusted or limited to one subgroup.

### 7.7 Use active and passive voice deliberately

Use active voice when the actor clarifies responsibility or sequence:

> We fitted the model after defining time zero.

Use passive voice when the object or procedure is the true topic or the actor is irrelevant:

> Blood samples were collected at baseline and week 12.

Never use passive voice to conceal who made a decision or assumption.

### 7.8 Control sentence length by structure, not by superstition

A long sentence can be clear if its hierarchy is obvious. A short sentence can be opaque if it contains undefined terms.

House triggers:

- Review sentences longer than 35 words.
- Strongly consider splitting sentences longer than 50 words.
- Review any sentence with more than one semicolon, nested parentheses or more than three distinct claims.

### 7.9 Avoid noun stacks

Weak:

> post-discharge child mortality risk prediction model calibration assessment

Better:

> assessment of how well the model calibrated predictions of child mortality after discharge

### 7.10 Use pronouns with explicit anchors

Replace vague “this”, “it” or “these” when the referent is not immediate.

Weak:

> This may explain the result.

Better:

> Differential loss to follow-up may explain the result.

## 8. Paragraph architectures

### 8.1 Introduction paragraph: CONTEXT–GAP–QUESTION–APPROACH

1. What is known and why it matters.
2. What is specifically unknown, inconsistent or methodologically unresolved.
3. The question the study addresses.
4. The design or analytical strategy, described at the level needed to understand the question.

### 8.2 Results paragraph: CLAIM–EVIDENCE–INTERPRETATION–BOUNDARY

1. Lead with the answer, not the analysis procedure.
2. Give the exact evidence.
3. Explain the direction or practical meaning.
4. State a material boundary, subgroup, sensitivity or uncertainty when needed.

### 8.3 Methods paragraph: PURPOSE–PROCEDURE–ASSUMPTION–OUTPUT

1. Why the method was needed.
2. What was done.
3. Which assumptions or design choices matter.
4. What quantity the method produced.

### 8.4 Discussion paragraph: FINDING–CONTEXT–EXPLANATION–IMPLICATION–LIMIT

1. State the finding.
2. Compare it with prior evidence.
3. Explain plausible mechanisms or reasons.
4. State the implication.
5. Mark what the evidence cannot establish.

### 8.5 Technical recommendation paragraph: PROBLEM–EVIDENCE–DECISION–CONDITION

1. Define the decision problem.
2. State the evidence supporting the recommendation.
3. Give the recommendation directly.
4. Specify conditions, resources, exceptions or monitoring needs.

## 9. Method explanation protocol

A method should never appear as a badge of sophistication. Explain why it exists in the analysis.

### Three-layer method sentence

1. **Problem:** what bias, uncertainty or inferential need existed.
2. **Method:** the exact method used.
3. **Function:** how it addressed the problem and what it estimated.

Example:

> Loss to follow-up was more common among children with severe illness, which could bias outcome comparisons. We therefore used inverse-probability-of-censoring weights to give greater influence to participants who resembled those who were lost. We then fitted a marginal structural model to estimate 12-month mortality under each treatment strategy, conditional on the measured censoring model being adequate.

### Methods that require especially careful explanation

- propensity scores and weighting;
- multiple imputation;
- multilevel models;
- machine-learning algorithms;
- mediation analysis;
- instrumental variables;
- target trial emulation;
- transportability methods;
- Bayesian models;
- latent-variable models;
- spatial models;
- interaction and effect modification;
- standardisation and g-computation.

For each, state the target quantity and major identification assumptions in language appropriate to the section.

## 10. Quantitative clarity protocol

### 10.1 Name the quantity

Do not write “the effect was 0.74” without naming whether this is a risk ratio, odds ratio, hazard ratio, mean difference, correlation, coefficient, area under the curve or another measure.

### 10.2 State the comparator

A percentage, ratio or difference is uninterpretable without a reference.

### 10.3 State the denominator and time

Prefer:

> During 12 months of follow-up, 18 of 240 children (7.5%) died.

Not:

> Mortality was 7.5%.

### 10.4 Distinguish percentage from percentage points

An increase from 10% to 15% is:

- a 5-percentage-point absolute increase;
- a 50% relative increase.

### 10.5 Pair relative and absolute effects where valid

Relative effects travel poorly when baseline risks vary. When data permit, report the absolute risk or difference alongside the relative measure.

### 10.6 Interpret uncertainty

Report the interval and explain material imprecision when necessary.

> The estimated risk ratio was 0.83 (95% CI 0.66 to 1.05), which is compatible with a meaningful reduction, little difference or a small increase.

Do not translate every interval mechanically. Interpret only when it helps the reader understand the decision uncertainty.

### 10.7 Do not use a P value as the finding

The finding is the estimated magnitude, direction and uncertainty. The P value, when reported, is supplementary evidence against a specified null model.

### 10.8 Reserve “significant”

Use “statistically significant” only when that is the intended meaning and the inferential framework justifies it. Use “important”, “large”, “clinically relevant” or “policy-relevant” only with an explicit criterion.

### 10.9 Do not convert odds into risks casually

An odds ratio is not a risk ratio. Avoid “times more likely” unless the measure and context support that interpretation.

### 10.10 Use “increase” and “decrease” carefully

For cross-sectional comparisons, prefer “higher” and “lower”. “Increased” and “decreased” can imply change over time or causation.

## 11. Epistemic calibration

### Descriptive evidence

Preferred verbs:

- observed
- recorded
- measured
- estimated
- occurred
- was higher or lower

### Associational evidence

Preferred verbs:

- was associated with
- differed by
- was correlated with
- co-occurred with
- showed a gradient with

Avoid without causal identification:

- caused
- led to
- produced
- prevented
- protected against
- drove
- resulted in

### Predictive evidence

Preferred verbs:

- predicted
- classified
- discriminated
- calibrated
- improved prediction

Do not infer causation from predictive importance, feature importance or model accuracy.

### Causal evidence

Causal verbs may be used only when the design, estimand and assumptions support a causal interpretation and the wording matches the intervention being estimated.

Preferred forms:

- reduced the risk of
- increased the mean outcome by
- the estimated effect of assigning...
- under the stated assumptions, the intervention would...

State the assumptions or design basis at an appropriate point. Do not use causal language merely because regression adjustment, propensity scores or machine learning were used.

### Mechanistic evidence

Use a strength ladder:

1. **Compatible with a mechanism**
2. **Supports a proposed mechanism**
3. **Provides evidence that the pathway contributes**
4. **Mediates part of the effect**, only under adequate mediation assumptions
5. **Is necessary, sufficient or causal**, only with evidence designed to establish that claim

### Policy or practice implications

Distinguish:

- evidence that identifies a problem;
- evidence that predicts who is at risk;
- evidence that an intervention works;
- evidence that implementation is feasible;
- a value-based recommendation.

Preferred calibrated language:

- may inform
- supports evaluating
- provides a rationale for
- suggests that programmes should consider
- is insufficient on its own to justify

## 12. Abstract engine

For a broad scientific journal, use six or seven moves:

1. **Problem:** the scientific or clinical issue in accessible language.
2. **Gap:** the precise limitation in current knowledge.
3. **Approach:** design, setting, population and question.
4. **Method:** only the method needed to interpret the result.
5. **Primary result:** magnitude, comparator and uncertainty.
6. **Secondary result or heterogeneity:** only if central.
7. **Meaning and boundary:** what changes, and what remains uncertain.

### Fillable template

> **[Problem]** remains **[consequence or scientific importance]**. However, **[specific knowledge or methodological gap]**. We **[study design and action]** among **[population and setting]** to estimate **[question or estimand]**. **[Primary result with exact measure and uncertainty]**. **[Central secondary or subgroup result]**. These findings **[calibrated implication]**, although **[main limitation or boundary]**.

### Abstract prohibitions

- Do not start with a ceremonial statement that could introduce any paper.
- Do not list every method.
- Do not use unexplained acronyms.
- Do not hide the direction or magnitude of the main result.
- Do not end with “further research is needed” unless the specific research need is named.
- Do not claim novelty, importance or impact without showing it.

## 13. Introduction engine

A strong introduction creates a narrowing funnel without a literature dump.

### Paragraph 1: field and consequence

State the problem in terms an adjacent scientist can understand. Define the central construct if the ordinary meaning differs from the technical meaning.

### Paragraph 2: what is known

Synthesize the strongest evidence. Organise by ideas, not by author chronology.

### Paragraph 3: exact gap

Name what prior studies could not distinguish, estimate, transport, measure or explain. The gap should logically require the present design.

### Final paragraph: question and contribution

State:

- study objective or hypothesis;
- population and setting;
- design;
- main analytical lens;
- what the study adds.

Avoid promises that the analysis cannot fulfil.

## 14. Methods engine

Methods should be reproducible but still readable.

For each subsection, use this order:

1. objective of the procedure;
2. data or participants;
3. definitions and time ordering;
4. analysis steps;
5. assumptions and diagnostics;
6. estimand or output;
7. software and reproducibility information.

Use equations when they increase precision. Introduce each equation with its purpose and define every symbol immediately.

Do not replace exact model specifications with vague prose. Accessibility and reproducibility are joint requirements.

## 15. Results engine

### Lead with answers

Weak heading:

> Multivariable analysis

Better heading:

> Wealth gradients in wasting differed by maternal height

### Result sequence

1. State the finding.
2. Provide the estimate and uncertainty.
3. Name the comparator, population and time.
4. Refer to the figure or table.
5. Give sensitivity or subgroup evidence when it changes interpretation.

### Do not mix interpretation prematurely

Results may explain what a measure means, but causal speculation and literature comparison normally belong in the Discussion.

### Avoid data narration

Do not repeat every number visible in a table. Select the quantities needed to answer the question and direct the reader to the complete display.

## 16. Discussion engine

### Opening paragraph

Answer the research question directly. Do not repeat the abstract word for word.

### Interpretation

Explain why the result may have occurred and how it changes prior understanding. Separate evidence from speculation.

### Comparison with prior work

Compare questions, populations, designs and effect measures, not merely whether studies “agree”.

### Strengths and limitations

Explain how each strength improves inference and how each limitation could affect direction, magnitude or generalisability. Avoid ceremonial lists.

### Implications

Link each implication to the evidence type. An observational association may justify surveillance or hypothesis testing but not automatically an intervention recommendation.

### Conclusion

State the narrowest defensible take-home message. Do not introduce new evidence, inflate the claim or end with a generic call for research.

## 17. Titles and headings

### Titles

Prefer:

- concrete subject;
- central relation, result or contribution;
- population or setting when necessary;
- no unnecessary acronym;
- no stacked catalogue of methods and findings;
- no claim stronger than the design.

Weak:

> A comprehensive multidimensional investigation of complex determinants...

Better:

> Acute wasting risk varies across maternal and socioeconomic contexts in four African countries

### Headings

Use answer-led headings in Results and decision-led headings in technical reports.

Headings should help a scanning reader recover the argument without reading every sentence.

## 18. Figures, tables and captions

A display should reduce cognitive load rather than transfer prose into a graphic.

### Figure title

State the take-home relation, not merely the chart type.

### Legend order

1. one-sentence message;
2. population and design;
3. panel-by-panel explanation;
4. statistical quantities and uncertainty;
5. abbreviation definitions.

### Table title

State who, what, where and when. Footnotes should define denominators, missingness, model adjustment and symbols.

## 19. Anti-slop firewall

Flag and usually remove:

- ceremonial openings;
- generic claims that a problem is “complex and multifaceted”;
- false contrast structures such as “not only X, but also Y” when no genuine contrast exists;
- forced sets of three;
- repeated “moreover”, “furthermore”, “notably” and “importantly”;
- motivational or promotional endings;
- inflated novelty language;
- anthropomorphic data or models;
- vague “robustness” claims;
- “delve”, “unpack”, “landscape”, “realm”, “paradigm” and “journey” used as decoration;
- excessive parentheses;
- rhetorical questions in original research articles;
- informal phrases, contractions, slang and idioms;
- empty intensifiers such as very, highly, remarkably and dramatically without a quantitative basis.

## 20. Fidelity audit

After rewriting, compare the original and revised text line by line.

Ask:

1. Did the claim class change?
2. Did any population, comparator, outcome or time period disappear?
3. Did an association become a cause?
4. Did an estimate become a fact?
5. Did uncertainty become less visible?
6. Did a limitation disappear or move too far from the claim?
7. Did the rewrite imply generalisation beyond the data?
8. Did a technical distinction collapse into a broad everyday word?
9. Were any citations detached from the claims they support?
10. Were numbers, units, signs or denominators altered?

If any answer is yes, restore the invariant before finalising.

## 22. Final standard

A successful LUCID-SCI passage allows:

- an adjacent scientist to understand the argument on the first reading;
- a specialist to recover the exact scientific claim and inspect its basis;
- a non-native English reader to follow the syntax without needless decoding;
- an editor to identify the contribution quickly;
- a reviewer to see uncertainty, assumptions and limits without searching for them.
