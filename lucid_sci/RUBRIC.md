# LUCID-SCI Quality Rubric

## 1. Hard-failure gates

A passage cannot be rated publication-ready if any of the following occurs:

1. The rewrite changes a descriptive or associational claim into a causal claim.
2. The population, comparator, outcome, time horizon or study design is materially misrepresented.
3. An effect estimate, unit, direction, denominator or uncertainty interval is altered.
4. A material limitation or assumption is removed to improve flow.
5. Technical terminology is replaced with a broader word that collapses an important distinction.
6. Missing information is invented.
7. A policy or clinical recommendation exceeds the evidence type.
8. A citation is left attached to a claim it does not support.
9. The prose implies generalisability beyond the sampled population or setting without justification.
10. The main result cannot be identified after reading the abstract or result paragraph.

Any hard failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

## 2. The 100-point LUCID-SCI score

### A. Scientific fidelity: 20 points

- 18–20: All semantic invariants preserved; no loss of design, estimand or limitation.
- 14–17: Minor compression but no material distortion.
- 8–13: Important qualification, definition or scope condition weakened.
- 0–7: Material scientific distortion.

### B. Claim calibration: 15 points

- 13–15: Verbs and implications match descriptive, associational, predictive, causal or mechanistic evidence.
- 10–12: Mostly calibrated; one or two phrases slightly overstate certainty.
- 5–9: Recurrent overclaiming or vague hedging.
- 0–4: Claim strength is systematically misleading.

### C. Reader orientation: 10 points

- 9–10: Purpose, gap and take-home message are immediately clear.
- 7–8: Reader can recover them with modest effort.
- 4–6: Important context or rationale appears late.
- 0–3: The text begins inside the method or terminology without orientation.

### D. Terminology management: 10 points

- 9–10: Essential terms are retained and bridged; unnecessary jargon and acronyms are absent.
- 7–8: A few terms need definition or replacement.
- 4–6: Dense unexplained terminology limits cross-disciplinary reading.
- 0–3: Status jargon or acronyms dominate the prose.

### E. Sentence architecture: 10 points

- 9–10: Clear subjects and verbs, controlled clauses, deliberate emphasis and few syntactic interruptions.
- 7–8: Mostly clear with occasional overload.
- 4–6: Frequent long interruptions, noun stacks or competing claims.
- 0–3: Sentences require repeated rereading.

### F. Paragraph coherence: 10 points

- 9–10: Each paragraph has one central purpose and a visible logic.
- 7–8: Logical but transitions or emphasis could improve.
- 4–6: Paragraphs mix context, method, result and speculation without hierarchy.
- 0–3: Sequence is difficult to reconstruct.

### G. Quantitative interpretability: 10 points

- 9–10: Measures, comparators, denominators, time and uncertainty are clear; magnitude is interpreted accurately.
- 7–8: Mostly complete but one element is difficult to recover.
- 4–6: Numbers are technically present but not interpretable without the table or methods.
- 0–3: Estimates are mislabeled, decontextualised or absent.

### H. Information hierarchy and navigation: 5 points

- 5: Title, abstract, headings and displays recover the argument.
- 4: Minor navigation problems.
- 2–3: Generic headings or buried take-home points.
- 0–1: No visible hierarchy.

### I. Tone and scholarly restraint: 5 points

- 5: Formal, natural, specific and measured.
- 4: Generally appropriate with isolated inflation or informality.
- 2–3: Promotional, bureaucratic or overly conversational.
- 0–1: Tone undermines credibility.

### J. Compression and economy: 5 points

- 5: Every sentence earns its place; repetition is purposeful.
- 4: Small redundancies.
- 2–3: Repeated ideas or long throat-clearing.
- 0–1: High word count with little added meaning.

## 3. Interpretation

- **90–100:** Cross-disciplinary journal ready, subject to scientific review.
- **80–89:** Strong; minor language and organisation revisions.
- **70–79:** Scientifically usable but still specialist-facing or uneven.
- **60–69:** Major clarity revision required.
- **Below 60:** Rebuild from the scientific spine rather than line-editing.

A score above 90 does not override a hard failure.

## 4. Review triggers

These are diagnostic flags, not automatic errors.

### Sentence flags

- more than 35 words;
- more than three distinct claims;
- subject and main verb separated by more than roughly 12 words;
- three or more nested prepositional phrases;
- nested parentheses;
- more than one semicolon;
- four-word or longer noun stack;
- vague opening with “It is”, “There is” or “This”;
- two or more undefined acronyms;
- main result appears only in a subordinate clause.

### Paragraph flags

- no identifiable topic sentence;
- more than one central claim;
- method appears before the reader knows the problem;
- limitation separated from the claim it qualifies;
- every sentence begins with a different topic;
- a citation list substitutes for synthesis;
- paragraph ends without interpretation or transition.

### Manuscript flags

- abstract has no numerical result;
- title lists methods rather than the scientific contribution;
- headings are generic, such as “Analysis” or “Results 2”;
- acronyms are defined in the abstract and then redefined differently;
- Discussion repeats results but does not interpret them;
- policy recommendation appears without implementation evidence;
- limitations are ceremonial rather than tied to possible bias.

## 5. Five-reader test

A manuscript should pass five simulated readers.

### Reader 1: the adjacent scientist

Can this reader state the question, main result and implication accurately after one reading?

### Reader 2: the methodological specialist

Can this reader identify the design, estimand, assumptions and uncertainty without detecting semantic loss?

### Reader 3: the decision-maker

Can this reader see what the evidence supports and what it does not support?

### Reader 4: the non-native English scientist

Can this reader follow the syntax without decoding idioms, ornamental language or unnecessary noun strings?

### Reader 5: the hostile reviewer

Can this reader locate the exact claim, evidence and limitation quickly enough to challenge them fairly?

## 6. Final release checklist

- [ ] Scientific checksum completed
- [ ] Main question stated
- [ ] Main result stated with magnitude and uncertainty
- [ ] Claim verbs calibrated
- [ ] Technical terms triaged
- [ ] Acronyms reduced
- [ ] Methods paired with purpose
- [ ] Paragraphs have one main job
- [ ] Results lead with answers
- [ ] Absolute and relative quantities paired where possible
- [ ] Limitations linked to their likely effect
- [ ] Title and headings are informative
- [ ] No promotional or AI-slop language
- [ ] No fabricated values or references
- [ ] Original and revised text passed fidelity comparison
