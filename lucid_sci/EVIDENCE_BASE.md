# Evidence Base and Design Rationale

LUCID-SCI is a synthesis of broad-readership journal guidance, plain-language standards, research on scientific readability and reader-oriented sentence design. The toolkit does not claim that every house heuristic is an official journal rule. Thresholds such as sentence-length flags are internal review triggers.

## 1. Nature: broad-readership scientific prose

Nature's formatting guide states that contributions should be written clearly and simply for readers in other disciplines and for readers who do not speak English as a first language. It advises avoiding technical jargon where possible, explaining unavoidable jargon and limiting abbreviations. Its summary paragraph is designed for readers outside the discipline and follows a recognisable sequence: basic field introduction, rationale, main conclusion and broader context.

Source: Nature, “Formatting guide”.  
https://www.nature.com/nature/for-authors/formatting-guide

## 2. Nature Communications: general introduction plus non-technical result summary

Nature Communications describes its readership as scientifically diverse and asks authors to avoid or explain field-specific language. Its article guidance says that the abstract should function as both a general introduction and a brief, non-technical summary of the principal results and implications.

Sources:  
https://www.nature.com/ncomms/submit/how-to-submit  
https://www.nature.com/ncomms/submit/article

## 3. Nature Medicine: concise primary research structure and specialist copy-editing

Nature Medicine limits research-article abstracts to 150 words and main text to 4,000 words, encouraging disciplined selection of information. After acceptance, subject specialists copy-edit text for clarity, readability, consistency, accuracy and journal style.

Sources:  
https://www.nature.com/nm/content  
https://www.nature.com/nm/submission-guidelines/production-process

## 4. Nature Reviews: accessible to advanced undergraduates and adjacent fields

Nature Reviews guidance states that articles should be accessible from advanced undergraduate level and to readers in related disciplines. It recommends enough background for non-experts, emphasis on implications rather than study-by-study description, plain language and restrained use of jargon, abbreviations and acronyms.

Source: Nature Reviews Nephrology, “Preparing your submission”.  
https://www.nature.com/nrneph/for-authors/preparing-your-submission

## 5. ISO plain science writing

ISO 24495-3:2026 establishes principles and guidelines for plain science writing for readers with varied education, backgrounds and interests. It builds on ISO 24495-1, whose governing orientation is that readers should be able to find, understand and use relevant information. ISO notes that Part 3 is aimed mainly at science writing for varied audiences rather than specialist journal articles, but that the principles can assist experts writing across disciplinary boundaries.

Sources:  
ISO 24495-3:2026, “Plain language — Part 3: Science writing”.  
https://www.iso.org/standard/86938.html  
ISO 24495-1:2023, “Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines”.  
https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html

## 6. Readability and jargon research

A corpus analysis of 709,577 abstracts from 123 journals found that scientific abstracts became less readable between 1881 and 2015, with the trend associated with increasing use of general scientific jargon. The study used readability formulas, which are useful as broad indicators but cannot by themselves assess logic, scientific fidelity or necessary technical complexity.

Plavén-Sigray P, Matheson GJ, Schiffler BC, Thompson WH. Research: The readability of scientific texts is decreasing over time. eLife. 2017;6:e27725.  
https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.27725

A study in Proceedings of the Royal Society B reported a negative association between specialised terminology in titles and abstracts and citation counts. This is observational evidence and should not be interpreted as proof that reducing jargon will itself increase citations.

Martínez A, Mammola S. Specialized terminology reduces the number of citations of scientific papers. Proc Biol Sci. 2021;288:20202581.  
https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2020.2581

The De-jargonizer study developed and validated an automated approach for identifying potentially difficult scientific vocabulary and demonstrated the substantial vocabulary difference between research abstracts and lay summaries. It supports terminology auditing but not indiscriminate deletion of technical words.

Rakedzon T, Segev E, Chapnik N, Yosef R, Baram-Tsabari A. Automatic jargon identifier for scientists engaging with the public and science communication educators. PLoS One. 2017;12:e0181742.  
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0181742

## 7. Claim calibration

A systematic review of highly shared health research and media reports found that causal wording often exceeded reviewers' assessment of the underlying strength of causal inference. LUCID-SCI therefore treats claim class as a semantic invariant and audits causal verbs separately from readability.

Haber N, Smith ER, Moscoe E, et al. Causal language and strength of inference in academic and media articles shared in social media. PLoS One. 2018;13:e0196346.  
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0196346

## 8. Reader-expectation sentence design

Gopen and Swan's reader-expectation approach emphasises keeping subjects close to verbs, using sentence openings to establish topic and context, placing important new information in positions of emphasis and maintaining old-to-new information flow. LUCID-SCI uses these ideas as structural principles rather than rigid word-count rules.

Gopen GD, Swan JA. The Science of Scientific Writing. American Scientist. 1990;78:550–558.  
https://www.americanscientist.org/blog/the-long-view/the-science-of-scientific-writing

## 9. Why the toolkit separates clarity from simplification

Technical terms can be efficient and exact within a community. The problem is not technicality itself but unmanaged assumptions about what the reader knows. LUCID-SCI therefore uses a four-way terminology decision: keep, bridge, replace or remove. This preserves terms such as confounding, mediation or transportability while explaining their function and limiting unnecessary vocabulary.

## 10. Scope

The toolkit is suitable for:

- original research manuscripts;
- reviews and perspectives;
- systematic reviews and evidence syntheses;
- statistical and epidemiological methods;
- technical reports;
- policy briefs written for technically literate readers;
- grant applications;
- protocols;
- figure legends and research summaries.

It should be adapted for legal drafting, patient information, informed consent, emergency communication and other settings with distinct regulatory or audience requirements.
